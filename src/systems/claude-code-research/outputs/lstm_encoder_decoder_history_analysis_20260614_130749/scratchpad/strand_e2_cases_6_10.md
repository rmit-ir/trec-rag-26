# Strand E2 — Case Studies 6–10: LSTM / Encoder–Decoder Systems in the Real World

**Author:** Research agent (RMIT IR Lab) · **Date:** 2026-06-14
**Scope:** Five case studies (6–10) for a "history of technology" seminar paper. Each entry gives: (a) the system and its builders; (b) the underlying architecture with a primary citation; (c) deployment scale/impact with cited figures; (d) case-specific societal/ethical dimensions; (e) full sources. Citations are labelled **[Primary paper]**, **[Official]**, **[Journalism]**, **[NGO/Policy]**, or **[Independent study]**. Marketing claims are explicitly flagged.

A note on architectural scope: cases 6 and 10 are classic LSTM/BLSTM/RNN-T systems. Cases 7 and 9 sit at the LSTM-to-Transformer transition — the *seq2seq encoder–decoder + attention* paradigm that LSTMs pioneered (Sutskever 2014; Bahdanau 2015) and that Transformers later inherited; I flag where the production system is LSTM-based vs. Transformer-based. Case 8 (surveillance) is treated as the deployment *lineage* of ASR/MT that the LSTM era supercharged; I am explicit about where the public evidence does and does not pin down the exact model family.

## CASE 6 — Microsoft conversational speech "human parity" (2016/2017)

**(a) What it is / who built it.** A research-grade automatic speech recognition (ASR) system for conversational telephone speech, built by Microsoft Research (W. Xiong, J. Droppo, X. Huang, F. Seide, M. Seltzer, A. Stolcke, D. Yu, G. Zweig and colleagues). It was benchmarked on the **NIST 2000 CTS test set**, comprising the *Switchboard* (strangers discussing an assigned topic) and *CallHome* (friends/family, open-ended) portions — the flagship conversational-ASR task since the 1990s Switchboard corpus. [Primary paper — Xiong et al. 2016/2017]

**(b) Architecture (primary citation).** The system is an ensemble of **convolutional and LSTM/BLSTM (bidirectional LSTM) acoustic models** — including CNN-BLSTM and ResNet variants — combined with a novel *spatial smoothing* method, *lattice-free MMI* acoustic training, multiple *recurrent neural network language models* (RNNLMs) for rescoring, and systematic *system combination*. The 2016 paper states: "The key to our system's performance is the use of various convolutional and LSTM acoustic model architectures, combined with a novel spatial smoothing method and lattice-free MMI acoustic training, multiple recurrent neural network language modeling approaches, and a systematic use of system combination." (Xiong et al., *Achieving Human Parity in Conversational Speech Recognition*, arXiv:1610.05256, MSR-TR-2016-71, rev. Feb. 2017.) The **2017 follow-up** (arXiv:1708.06073) "adds a CNN-BLSTM acoustic model" and drove WER to 5.1%. This is an LSTM-era system, not a Transformer one. [Primary papers]

**(c) Deployment scale / impact (cited figures).**
- **2016 result:** automated system 5.8% WER on Switchboard and 11.0% on CallHome, vs. **human professional transcribers at 5.9% (Switchboard) and 11.3% (CallHome)** — i.e., the machine edged past the human benchmark for the first time on this task. (arXiv:1610.05256, abstract.) [Primary paper]
- **2017 result:** 5.1% WER on the 2000 Switchboard evaluation set and 9.8% on CallHome, described by Microsoft as "a new industry milestone, substantially surpassing the accuracy we [previously] achieved." (Xiong et al., arXiv:1708.06073; Microsoft Research blog, 20 Aug 2017.) [Primary paper + Official]
- Note this is a **research benchmark, not a deployed product metric.** WER figures are research-system numbers on a curated 8 kHz telephone test set; they are not a claim about real-world product accuracy. The techniques fed Microsoft's commercial speech stack (Cognitive Services / Cortana / dictation), but the 5.9%/5.1% numbers are benchmark results.

**(d) What "human parity" did and didn't mean.** This is the case's central historiographical point.
- **Did mean:** on this *specific test set and this metric (WER)*, the system's aggregate error rate matched or beat the measured error rate of professional transcribers Microsoft itself commissioned. It was a genuine ASR-history milestone, widely reported as such.
- **Did *not* mean:** human-level *understanding* of speech. Microsoft's own follow-up study (Stolcke & Droppo, "Comparing Human and Machine Errors in Conversational Speech," Interspeech 2017) found that machine and human errors have **different distributions** — the machine and humans confuse different words, and the machine lacks the contextual/semantic robustness humans bring (speaker intent, disfluency handling, background knowledge). "Human parity" was a parity of *aggregate WER on a benchmark*, not of comprehension, robustness to accents/noise, or out-of-domain generalisation. The 2018 ICASSP paper re-measured human WER more carefully and reported 5.9% human vs. 5.8% machine — "not statistically different." [Primary papers]
- **Ethical/critical dimension:** "human parity" became a contested marketing-vs-science boundary object. Critics (e.g., commentary on Language Log, ACM/the morning paper) cautioned that the phrase invites over-generalisation — equating a narrow benchmark win with broad human equivalence, which feeds hype cycles and can mislead procurement decisions in high-stakes ASR uses (courtrooms, medical dictation, accessibility) where real-world WER is far higher and error *types* matter. The case is a textbook example of why benchmark parity ≠ deployment parity.

## CASE 7 — Low-resource / endangered-language machine translation

**(a) Systems / who built them.** Three intertwined efforts:
- **Meta AI — No Language Left Behind (NLLB-200)** (2022; peer-reviewed in *Nature* 2024). A single multilingual MT model covering **200 languages**, three times as many low-resource as high-resource languages, with open release of models, the **FLORES-200** benchmark, and mined data. (NLLB Team / Costa-jussà et al.)
- **Google — "Building Machine Translation Systems for the Next Thousand Languages"** (Bapna, Caswell, Kreutzer, Firat, van Esch, Siddhant et al., 2022), which underpinned Google Translate's 24-language "zero-resource" expansion (May 2022) and the 110-language expansion (2024).
- **Masakhane** — a grassroots, pan-African, open-source participatory-research collective (Orife, Kreutzer, ∀ et al.), which reframed low-resource MT as *participatory* rather than purely data-centric.

**(b) Architecture (primary citations).** These are **Transformer encoder–decoder (seq2seq) NMT** systems — the direct architectural descendant of the LSTM seq2seq+attention lineage (Sutskever 2014; Bahdanau 2015), now with self-attention. Specifically:
- **NLLB-200** uses **Sparsely Gated Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)** Transformer models (the published flagship is a 54.5B-parameter MoE; a dense 3.3B baseline is also reported), plus **LASER3** distilled multilingual sentence encoders for bitext mining, Expert Output Masking (EOM) regularisation, and curriculum learning. (NLLB Team, "Scaling neural machine translation to 200 languages," *Nature* 631, 2024, doi:10.1038/s41586-024-07335-x.) [Primary paper, peer-reviewed]
- **Google's "next 1000 languages"** combines supervised and **self-supervised / zero-resource** monolingual training (MASS-style and back-translation) on Transformer NMT. (Bapna, Caswell et al., arXiv:2205.03983, 2022.) [Primary paper]
- **Masakhane** is methodological (benchmarks, datasets, JoeyNMT-based Transformer baselines) rather than a single model. (Orife et al., "Masakhane — Machine Translation For Africa," 2020; ∀ et al., "Participatory Research for Low-resourced Machine Translation: A Case Study in African Languages," EMNLP Findings 2020.) [Primary papers]

**(c) Deployment scale / impact (cited figures).**
- **NLLB-200**: "performs, as a mean, **44% better** than the previous state-of-the-art systems" (measured as **+7.3 spBLEU** average on FLORES-101 devtest vs. nearest SOTA), while covering 200 languages and >30,000 additional directions; +7.0 spBLEU on the 87-language FLORES-101 subset. Models/data released openly (non-commercial) on fairseq. (Nature 2024.) [Primary paper]
- **Real-world uptake:** four months after launch, **Wikimedia reported NLLB-200 was the 3rd-most-used MT engine by Wikipedia editors, accounting for 3.8% of all published translations**, with the lowest deletion rate (0.13%). (Nature 2024, citing Wikimedia Research.) [Primary paper, citing official Wikimedia data]
- **Google Translate**: the 1000-languages work enabled adding **24 new languages in 2022** (e.g., Lingala, Bhojpuri, Quechua) via zero-resource methods, and **110 languages in 2024**, framed as serving languages spoken by hundreds of millions previously unserved. (Bapna/Caswell 2022; Google blog, 27 Jun 2024.) [Primary paper + Official]
- **Live coverage:** Live Transcribe / Translate marketing cites "over 70 languages and more than 80% of the world's population" for ASR (see Case 10); NLLB targets the long tail beyond that.

**(d) Societal & ethical dimensions specific to this case.**
- **Benefits:** for many low-resource communities, NLLB-200 is "**one of the first models designed to support translation into or out of their languages**," with the most significant impact projected in education and access to Wikipedia/news, "challenging Western-centric modes of knowledge production." (Nature 2024.) [Primary paper]
- **Quality/representation gap & the "can't-vet" risk:** independent and ethics scholarship stresses that MT quality for low-resource languages is far below high-resource pairs, and that deploying *plausible-but-wrong* MT into communities that **cannot themselves vet the output** is a distinct harm — it can corrupt the very digital corpora future models train on, misrepresent the language, and erode trust. Haroutunian (ACL SRW 2022, "Ethical Considerations for Low-resourced Machine Translation," Armenian case study) argues low-resource MT must be treated as a **language-specific, not language-agnostic** task, developed *with* stakeholders, lest improved MT cause harms (e.g., misinformation, displacement of human translators, data extraction without consent). [Independent/academic]
- **Toxicity:** NLLB's own evaluation built **ETOX** and found "added toxicity" in translations traceable to misaligned mined bitext; filtering ~30% of parallel sentences cut added toxicity and improved chrF++ by ~5%. (Nature 2024.) This is a concrete, paper-documented harm and mitigation. [Primary paper]
- **Data colonialism / extraction:** Masakhane's participatory model is an explicit ethical counter-stance — insisting African-language NLP be "OPEN SOURCE, CONTINENT-WIDE, DISTRIBUTED," led by and accountable to speaker communities, rather than data mined by external labs. (Masakhane / EMNLP-Findings 2020.) [Primary paper]
- **Data sourcing bias:** the Nature paper notes much existing low-resource bitext is dominated by religious texts (e.g., the Bible), skewing domain coverage — a representational limitation. [Primary paper]

## CASE 8 — Automated translation/transcription in state surveillance (dual-use NLP)

**(a) Systems / who built them.** Two documented threads:
- **NSA speech-to-text / "Google for Voice" (USA).** A succession of in-house and contractor ASR/MT systems for intercepted voice, documented from the Snowden archive by *The Intercept* (Dan Froomkin, 2015; Ava Kofman, 2018): **RHINEHART** (keyword voice search, rolled out 2004, used "throughout the NSA/CSS Enterprise"); **VoiceRT** (Baghdad, "index and tag 1 million cuts per day"); **SPIRITFIRE** (operational ~2013, "speech-to-text keyword search and paired dialogue transcription"); **EViTAP** (fully automated news monitoring combining ASR + machine translation in six languages — Arabic, Mandarin, Russian, Spanish, English, Farsi); plus contractor systems (BBN **Byblos**, Dragon). [Journalism, based on primary leaked docs]
- **Uyghur-targeted language/biometric surveillance (China, Xinjiang).** The **Integrated Joint Operations Platform (IJOP)** mass-surveillance system documented by Human Rights Watch (Maya Wang, "China's Algorithms of Repression," 2019) via reverse-engineering the IJOP police app; and the 2023 HRW finding on a police master list of ~50,000 "violent and terrorist" multimedia files used to flag Uyghur content. iFLYTEK (a leading Chinese speech-AI firm) has been widely reported and US-sanctioned in connection with Xinjiang voice/biometric programs. [NGO/Policy + Journalism]

**(b) Architecture (with caveats).** This is the case where architecture is *least* pinned to a specific model family in public sources, and I flag that explicitly:
- The NSA systems described in the 2015 Intercept piece predate the LSTM-ASR breakthrough; the article itself notes that by 2015 "Enormous processors, tuned as 'deep neural networks' … produce much cleaner transcripts," marking the transition into the deep-learning (and soon LSTM/RNN) ASR era. EViTAP is an explicit **ASR → machine-translation pipeline** (an encoder–decoder-style cascade in function, whatever the era's internals). The exact 2016+ model architectures are classified. **Framing for the paper:** treat NSA STT as the *deployment lineage* that LSTM/seq2seq ASR and NMT later supercharged — bulk, searchable, multilingual transcription at scale. [Journalism]
- For IJOP, HRW's reverse-engineering documents *data collection and flagging logic* (biometrics, phone/app monitoring, "violent audio-visual content" detection) rather than the speech/translation model internals; the speech-recognition component (e.g., iFLYTEK's) is documented via procurement and corporate reporting, not architecture papers. [NGO/Policy]

**(c) Deployment scale / impact (cited figures).**
- **NSA:** VoiceRT "designed to index and tag **1 million cuts per day**"; HLT goal that "a single analyst will be able to sort through **millions of cuts per day**"; documented operational use in Iraq, Afghanistan, Mexico and Latin America (Real Time Regional Gateway). Critically, the Intercept frames the shift as the NSA "**entered the era of bulk listening**" — automated STT removed the human-listener bottleneck that had limited surveillance to "a tiny percentage of the total traffic." (Froomkin, *The Intercept*, 5 May 2015.) [Journalism]
- **Xinjiang/IJOP:** the system targets a population of **13 million Turkic Muslims** in Xinjiang; HRW documents mass collection of personal data and flagging of behaviours with "no clear relationship to terrorism." (HRW 2019.) The 2023 HRW report documents a ~**50,000-file** master list used to flag Uyghur/ethnic content on phones. [NGO/Policy]

**(d) Ethics of dual-use NLP (case-specific).**
- **Bulk surveillance economics.** The core ethical pivot, in the words of Stanford's Jennifer Granick quoted by the Intercept: "the economics of surveillance have totally changed." Cheap, accurate STT/MT turns *targeted* interception into *bulk, retrospective, keyword-searchable* surveillance — a qualitative change in state power, with "no apparent public oversight, hearings or legislative action." [Journalism]
- **Opacity / due-process harm.** UC Davis's Phillip Rogaway (quoted by the Intercept) warns that when NLP flags someone as "interesting," there may be "no human-understandable explanation," making it "impossible to understand the contours of the surveillance apparatus by which one is judged" — an accountability and chilling-effects harm specific to ML-driven selection. [Journalism]
- **Ethnic targeting / repression.** The Xinjiang case is the paradigmatic dual-use harm: the *same* ASR/MT capability that powers accessibility (Case 10) and language preservation (Case 7) is repurposed to identify, track and detain an ethnic minority by language and behaviour. Language technology becomes an instrument of what HRW calls "algorithms of repression." [NGO/Policy]
- **Dual-use framing for the paper:** Cases 6, 7 and 10 show the benevolent face of LSTM/seq2seq speech-and-translation; Case 8 is the same technical stack inverted. The ethical lesson is that capability is *governance-neutral* — the deployment context (oversight, consent, target population) determines whether bulk transcription/translation is accessibility or oppression.

## CASE 9 — Neural MT & text generation in disinformation / influence operations

**(a) Systems / actors.** Not a single system but the *capability class*: cheap multilingual NMT (Cases 7–8 lineage) plus neural text generation (GPT-2, 2019; GPT-3, 2020), studied for misuse by (i) **OpenAI** itself (staged-release of GPT-2 and the accompanying social-impacts report), (ii) **Georgetown's CSET** (Buchanan, Lohn, Musser, Sedova, *Truth, Lies, and Automation*, 2021; and the 2023 CSET×OpenAI forecasting report), and (iii) the **Stanford Internet Observatory** (DiResta et al.), which documents real coordinated-inauthentic-behavior (CIB) takedowns including multilingual operations.

**(b) Architecture (primary citations).** Transformer decoder-only LMs (GPT-2/3) for generation; Transformer encoder–decoder NMT for translation — again the seq2seq/attention lineage descended from LSTM seq2seq. The relevant *research artifacts* are policy/threat-model papers rather than architecture papers:
- OpenAI, "Release Strategies and the Social Impacts of Language Models," arXiv:1908.09203 (Nov 2019) — the GPT-2 staged-release report. [Primary report]
- Buchanan et al., "Truth, Lies, and Automation: How Language Models Could Change Disinformation," CSET, May 2021 — the canonical GPT-3 disinformation study. [Primary report]

**(c) Scale / impact (cited figures).**
- **GPT-2 staged release (2019):** OpenAI deliberately released GPT-2 in stages — 124M (Feb), 355M (May), 774M (Aug), then the full **1.5-billion-parameter** model (Nov 5, 2019) — citing "concerns about malicious applications of the technology" including "generating fake news content, impersonating [others] … producing abusive or faked content." The report's actor model tiers risk into low-skill, moderate-skill, and "**Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs)**: highly skilled and well-resourced groups, like state-sponsored actors." This is the **founding episode of "responsible release" debate** in NLP. (arXiv:1908.09203.) [Primary report]
- **GPT-3 disinformation (CSET 2021):** with *free research access* to GPT-3, CSET evaluated six disinformation tasks (narrative reiteration, elaboration, manipulation, seeding, wedging, persuasion). Key findings: GPT-3 "excels with little human involvement" at reiteration; a human-machine team can "craft credible targeted messages in just **minutes**"; and in a persuasion experiment, "after seeing **five short messages written by GPT-3 and selected by humans, the percentage of survey respondents opposed to sanctions on China doubled**." CSET concluded GPT-3 can produce "moderate- to high-quality messages at a scale much greater than what has come before," well "within the capacity of foreign governments … such as China and Russia." Importantly, their results are framed as a **low-end estimate** of adversary capability. (Buchanan et al., CSET 2021.) [Primary report]
- **The translation multiplier:** cheap NMT lets a single operation localise content across languages, scaling CIB beyond a campaign's native-speaker staff. Stanford Internet Observatory / Graphika analyses of platform takedowns (e.g., the 2022 pro-Western covert-IO study, and earlier Russia/Iran/China network reports) document **machine-translation artifacts and cross-lingual content** as recurring fingerprints of coordinated operations. Meta reported disrupting **200+ covert influence networks** by end-2022. [NGO/Independent + Official]

**(d) Societal & ethical dimensions specific to this case.**
- **The cost/scale shift.** The throughline from GPT-2 → GPT-3 research is that generation+translation collapse the *marginal cost* of producing fluent, localized, on-narrative text toward zero, while detection stays hard. CSET's mitigation insight is telling: because machine text "blends in so well with human writing, the best way to thwart" misuse is to target the *propagation infrastructure* (fake accounts) "rather than … determining the authorship of the text itself" — i.e., content-level detection is a losing game. [Primary report]
- **Targeted manipulation / bias.** CSET flagged that for "narrative wedging," GPT-3 "deploys stereotypes and racist language … a tendency of particular concern" — the model's training biases become a *feature* for demographically targeted division. [Primary report]
- **The staged-release precedent and its critics.** OpenAI's GPT-2 staged release seeded a lasting governance debate: was withholding the model responsible risk-management or unfalsifiable hype? The report documents partner research (Cornell on human susceptibility; Middlebury's CTEC on extremist misuse) and the eventual finding of "minimal evidence of misuse" of the smaller models — a key data point for the paper's "did the predicted harms materialise?" discussion. [Primary report; MIT Tech Review coverage]
- **Dual-use continuity with Case 8.** The same multilingual NMT that serves low-resource communities (Case 7) is the disinformation translation layer here — reinforcing the governance-neutral-capability thesis.

## CASE 10 — Clinical / accessibility deployment: Google Live Transcribe (2019) + (secondary) Nuance DAX

I picked **Live Transcribe** as the primary, best-sourced accessibility example (clear primary research-blog source, named architecture, billion-scale impact, peer-adjacent UX research with Gallaudet), and include **Nuance Dragon Medical / DAX** as a corroborating high-stakes clinical example with strong (but largely vendor-sourced) figures — flagged as marketing where appropriate.

### 10A — Google Live Transcribe / Live Caption (accessibility at scale)

**(a) What it is / who built it.** **Live Transcribe** — a free Android app providing real-time speech-to-text captions for deaf and hard-of-hearing users — built by Google researchers **Chet Gnegy, Dimitri Kanevsky (himself deaf), Justin S. Paul, Sagar Savla** and the Android Accessibility team, **in collaboration with Gallaudet University** (the US university for the deaf). Launched 4 Feb 2019. The on-device sibling **Live Caption** (captions any audio on the device) shipped on Pixel 4 / Android 10 the same year. [Official + Primary research blog]

**(b) Architecture (primary citation).** A hybrid: **cloud ASR** (Google Cloud Speech, the production LSTM/RNN-Transducer (RNN-T) recognizer of that era) for accuracy, gated by an **on-device neural speech detector** ("an image-like model, similar to our published VGGish model," built on AudioSet) that manages network connections and minimises data usage. Google open-sourced the speech-engine library in Aug 2019. (Google Research blog, "Real-time Continuous Transcription with Live Transcribe," 4 Feb 2019.) The underlying Google ASR of 2019 was the on-device **RNN-T (LSTM-based)** recognizer (He et al., "Streaming End-to-end Speech Recognition for Mobile Devices," ICASSP 2019) — squarely an LSTM/encoder-decoder production system. [Primary research blog + Primary paper for the ASR engine]

**(c) Scale / impact (cited figures).**
- Targets the **WHO-estimated 466 million** deaf/hard-of-hearing people worldwide; supports "**over 70 languages and dialects** and more than **80% of the world's population**." (Google Research blog, 2019.) [Official/research]
- **Live Transcribe surpassed 1 billion downloads** (reported Dec 2023, e.g., Gallaudet). [Independent/secondary — flag: download count is a Play Store metric, not an accuracy metric]
- Pre-installed on Pixel devices; replaces or supplements expensive, pre-scheduled human services (CART/Palantypist/STTR) that the blog notes "can be prohibitively expensive and often require to be scheduled far in advance." [Official/research]

**(d) Societal & ethical dimensions.**
- **Accessibility benefit:** democratises real-time captioning — impromptu conversation access that previously required booking a human captioner — a genuine equity gain for the d/Deaf community, co-designed *with* Gallaudet (a participatory-design strength). [Official/research]
- **Error & confidence risks:** Google's own UX research found displaying word/phrase-level **confidence** was "distracting … without providing conversational value," so it was *not* shown — meaning users may not know when a caption is wrong. The blog warns "relying solely on transcription can have pitfalls that can lead to miscommunication" (the "cocktail party"/noise problem). In high-stakes settings (medical, legal) silent ASR errors are a real harm. [Primary research blog — notable that the vendor itself flags this]
- **Bias:** ASR is documented to have higher error rates for some accents, dialects, and AAVE speakers (Koenecke et al., PNAS 2020, "Racial disparities in automated speech recognition") — a fairness concern directly relevant to an accessibility tool meant to serve *everyone*. [Independent study]

### 10B — Nuance Dragon Medical / DAX Copilot (clinical documentation) — corroborating example

**(a/b) System / architecture.** **Nuance Dragon Medical** (medical ASR dictation) and **DAX / DAX Copilot** (ambient clinical documentation that listens to the doctor–patient conversation and drafts the note). Built by **Nuance Communications** (acquired by **Microsoft**, 2021–22). Architecturally it combines **medical ASR** (LSTM/Transformer speech recognition lineage) with **seq2seq summarization/generation** to turn dialogue into a structured note; DAX Copilot (GA Sep 2023) added LLM generation. (Nuance/Microsoft; healthcare press.) [Official + Journalism]

**(c) Scale / impact (cited figures — mostly vendor-sourced, flagged).**
- Dragon Medical platform used by **>550,000 users worldwide**; Nuance "trusted by **77% of U.S. hospitals**." (Nuance/Atrium PRNewswire, Oct 2023.) **[Vendor figure — marketing]**
- DAX Copilot: **>400 healthcare organizations** within a year of GA (Microsoft blog, 26 Sep 2024); vendor-claimed **~7 minutes saved per encounter**, "5 additional appointments per clinic day," "70% reduction in feelings of burnout." **[Vendor figures — marketing]**
- *Independent/site-specific corroboration:* Stanford Health Care reported surpassing **1 million DAX Copilot clinical notes** (2026); Novant Health internal reporting cited **~900 clinicians, 550,000+ encounters**. A peer-reviewed study (Tierney et al., *NEJM Catalyst* / PMC10990544, 2024) found "positive trends in provider engagement **at no risk to patient safety**," with only **5 of ~36,000 patients (0.014%) opting out** of DAX use. [Independent/peer-reviewed for the safety/opt-out figures]

**(d) Ethics / risks (high-stakes clinical).**
- **Error in high-stakes settings:** an ambient system that *drafts* the medico-legal record introduces risks of hallucinated or omitted clinical facts; safety depends on the clinician reviewing/finalising every note (the human-in-the-loop is load-bearing). The peer-reviewed signal ("no risk to patient safety") is reassuring but single-site and early. [Independent study + risk analysis]
- **Consent & privacy:** ambient recording of patient conversations raises consent questions (hence the documented opt-out mechanism). [Independent study]
- **Bias:** clinical ASR error rates vary by accent/dialect, risking documentation-quality disparities across patient and clinician populations (cf. Koenecke et al. 2020). [Independent study]
- **Marketing-vs-evidence caution:** the headline productivity/burnout figures are overwhelmingly **vendor-internal**; the strongest *independent* evidence is the small-but-peer-reviewed safety/satisfaction studies, which the paper should weight accordingly.

## Cross-cutting synthesis (for the paper's argument)

1. **Architectural arc.** Cases 6 and 10A are the *clearest* LSTM/BLSTM/RNN-T deployments (telephone ASR; mobile streaming ASR). Cases 7, 9, and 10B sit on the seq2seq encoder–decoder + attention continuum that LSTMs pioneered and Transformers inherited — so they belong in an "LSTM/encoder-decoder history" if framed as that lineage rather than as pure-LSTM artifacts. Case 8 is the deployment context (bulk ASR/MT) that the LSTM era industrialised; its exact internals are classified.
2. **The benchmark-vs-deployment gap** (Case 6) and the **marketing-vs-evidence gap** (Cases 7 NLLB claims, 10B DAX figures) are recurring methodological themes: "human parity," "44% better," and "70% less burnout" are all real numbers attached to narrow conditions.
3. **Governance-neutral capability / dual-use** is the strongest cross-case thesis: the *same* multilingual ASR/MT stack is accessibility (10A), language revitalisation (7), bulk surveillance (8), and disinformation translation (9). The deployment context — consent, oversight, target population — not the architecture, determines the ethics.
